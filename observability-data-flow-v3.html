A live football match. At 12:11 UTC a CDN edge begins failing for a subset of viewers. We follow one of them.

intent_seconds = 60 · active_seconds = 47 · 3 rebuffers totalling 13,040 ms · no ads in this minute. The viewer is having a bad minute — formally an impacted minute, and note what that means: all 60 of its opportunity seconds count as impacted, not just the 13 spent buffering. Minute health is seconds-weighted, not seconds-resolved. Thousands of viewers on the same edge are having the same minute.

The asymmetry is deliberate

The client is a measurement instrument, not a decision-maker. It classifies its own minute against a policy and posts it. It never evaluates a cohort predicate, never learns a population statistic, and is never told its derived dimensions — which is exactly why forensic targeting has to be resolved server-side and ride the ACK (stage 10).

The scaling problem

Event volume is a property of the player, not of the viewing. A player release that raises timeupdate frequency from 3s to 1s triples your telemetry bill and changes nothing about what anyone learns.

Reduction so far

10,230 B → 342 B · 30×, with no loss of the measurement substrate the supported KPIs need. Diagnostic precision is deliberately discarded, and more goes in the next stage: rebuffer_ms = 13040 is exact here because it was measured on-device at full resolution, but packing will reduce it to a bucket.

One flag you will not see here

This viewer recovered from all three stalls, so the minute is REBUFFER. Had any single stall run past 30 seconds the minute would carry STALL_TIMEOUT instead, REBUFFER would be cleared, and intent seconds would stop accruing — so a viewer who walked away from a spinning player cannot quietly dominate the impact ranking in stage 10.

Note the two bitmaps

content_issues and ad_issues are separate. A single bitmap plus a seconds split would let an ad-server failure register as content degradation. CDN_SWITCH sits in ctx_change_flags, outside the issue bitmap, so "any issue bit set" never misfires on a routine failover.

04

Packing: 342 B to 24 B

on device

Field names, quotes, and repeated identifiers are the bulk of the JSON, and varint encoding shrinks what is left. The wire format carries none of them.

packed ExperienceMinute · byte layout24 B

Tap any byte to see what it holds. Dotted terms elsewhere on the page work the same way.

time & sequence — delta from batch base (3 B) context ref (1 B) content seconds & issues (3 B) ad seconds & issues (3 B) issue counters, bucketed (8 B) context-change flags (1 B) bitrate bucket + peak-rendition seconds (2 B) reserved / alignment (3 B)

This is a proposed encoding, not a measured one. The field widths, varint sizes and bucket boundaries below are internally consistent and need implementing and measuring before any of them is quoted. Absent by design: playback_id, session_id, and all three policy versions. They are constant across the batch, so they live once in the batch header rather than 5× in the records.

where the 318 bytes went
JSON keys & syntax191 B−191
IDs hoisted to header88 B−88
ts/seq → deltas24−24
ms → bucketed varints15−15
remaining payload24 B24
The one real loss here

rebuffer_ms goes from exact (13040) to a log-scale bucket (bucket 11 = 12.8–16.4s). Bucketing is what allows any later re-thresholding at all — a bare boolean could never be recovered. But bucket resolution is the permanent floor on retrospective analysis.

Why 24 B and not 8 B

The record is still 24 B after quality measurement was added, because bitrate and peak-rendition seconds went into reserved bytes. That is what reserve is for. Three remain — adding a field later without a schema migration across millions of unupgradeable CTV devices is worth more than three bytes.

What actually crosses the wire

device → backend

One upload per minute, staggered across the minute so a million devices do not all transmit at :00, over a connection that stays open. The cadence is a dial, and where it is set decides both the bill and the detection floor.

Clock correction, config pointer, and forensic targeting all ride the response. No polling loop, no second control channel.

Cadence is the dial

Every 5 minutes: ~23 B/min. The 56 B header amortises over five records and zstd has enough material to work with. But it puts a 5-minute floor under detection, which would eat the entire latency budget before the detector ran once.

Every minute — what this deployment does: ~78 B/min, roughly 3.4× the bytes, each device transmitting in its own staggered slot. A single 24 B record barely compresses, and per-request framing no longer has four siblings to share with. You are buying detection latency with bytes. Targeted playbacks drop to 10s and pay more again.

The honest compression ladder

Raw events, uncompressed10,230 B1×
Raw events, zstd batched1,600 B6.4×
Semantic JSON34230×
Packed record24 B · 426×
On the wire, all-in78 B · 131×

Do not quote 131×. And note the last two rows: the wire figure is larger than the packed record, because header and framing dominate once you send every minute. Production event pipelines already batch and compress, so the fair wire-to-wire comparison is 1,600 B → 78 B ≈ 20× for this worked example — one synthetic minute, excluding attempts, intervals, context definitions, retries and control traffic. It is not a system-level compression figure, and the 80 B/minute planning number stays primary. The claim worth defending is not a multiple at all: it is that volume now scales with viewing minutes instead of with player chattiness.

Where the 80 B/minute planning figure comes from

It is not padding. ~78 B/min is what a 1-minute cadence actually costs, and the architecture spec rounds it to 80. At 1M concurrent that is 1.3 MB/s. The 23 B/min figure people remember belongs to the 5-minute cadence nobody is running.

backend · ingest

Requested ≠ served

The client asked for edge-b. It was served by edge-b7 after the failover at 12:11:42. Only the server knows the second fact, and the second fact is the one the incident turns on.

OLAP storage: the raw minute tier

backend · storage

Short retention, per-playback granularity, heavily codec-compressed. No dashboard or detector reads it on the hot path; drill-down, exact seconds-weighted confirmation, and interval joins do.

TTL          minute_ts + INTERVAL 21 DAY;

There are no per-column indexes. The sort key is the index, it is sparse — one entry per 8,192-row granule — and it only accelerates filters on a prefix of itself. Sorting by context_key before playback_id puts thousands of identical values in a run, which compresses them to almost nothing. It does not turn a broad cohort query into a single range read: context_key is a surrogate, so "all of edge-b" is thousands of scattered keys.

So how is a drill-down fast?

Filter on cdn and the engine resolves it against context_dim first — 180k rows, in memory, sub-millisecond — then pushes the matching keys into the cube scan. It is quick because the thing being scanned is small, not because anything is indexed. A detector window is ~210k cube rows. The cube's real job is to make indexing unnecessary: 1.44 billion per-playback rows a day would force an indexing strategy, and aggregating at write time buys the freedom not to have one.

The interval tier: what was watched

backend · storage

Minutes know quality but not content. Intervals know content but not minutes. Watch time, completion and per-title reporting all live here — on 90-day retention, because these are business numbers, not incident numbers.

Two completion numbers on purpose. max_position_pct reads 100% for someone who seeks to the credits; watched_pct does not. Product teams want the gap between them. Asset length lives in an asset dimension table, never on every row.

content_daily · the reporting rollup
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW content_daily_mv TO content_daily AS
SELECT
  toDate(start_ts) AS day, asset_id, asset_type,
  device_class, country, cdn,          -- reduced dims
  sum(active_ms)                      AS watch_ms,
  sum(duration_ms)                    AS elapsed_ms,
  avg(watched_pct)                    AS avg_watched_pct,
  countIf(end_reason = 'completed')  AS completions,
  count()                             AS intervals,
  uniqCombinedState(playback_id)     AS playbacks
FROM playback_interval JOIN context_dim USING (context_key)
GROUP BY day, asset_id, asset_type, device_class, country, cdn;
top titles by watch hours
SELECT asset_id,
       sum(watch_ms)/3.6e6                 AS watch_hours,
       uniqCombinedMerge(playbacks)       AS unique_plays,
       sum(completions)/sum(intervals)    AS completion_rate,
       avg(avg_watched_pct)               AS avg_pct_watched
FROM content_daily
WHERE day >= today() - 30 AND asset_type = 'content'
GROUP BY asset_id
ORDER BY watch_hours DESC LIMIT 50;

Ad exposure comes out of the same table via asset_type, with no second pipeline.

Two watch-time numbers that will never match

Interval watch time does not equal summed minute active_seconds. Intervals measure playback; minutes apply eligibility rules and exclude paused time. They measure different things deliberately. Publish one definition of each and say so — otherwise it arrives as a bug report in month two.

No client session summaries

Audience and engagement derive from this tier and from attempts, with unique viewers from a sketch. A client-emitted session summary is lost exactly when sessions end badly — app killed, TV unplugged — so audience figures built on it would be biased low in precisely the cohorts having problems. If a terminal session record is sent, its job is completeness checking, never metric production.

The cube: where the read path actually lives

backend · storage

A materialized view collapses per-playback rows into per-context aggregates at write time. 16,700 rows/s becomes 700 — a 24× row reduction and a 15× byte reduction, with retention going from 21 days to years.

minute_cube · AggregatingMergeTree, fed by MV
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW minute_cube_mv TO minute_cube AS
SELECT
  minute_ts,
  context_key,
  policy_key,
  sum(content_intent_s)                                AS opportunity_s,
  sumIf(content_intent_s, content_issues  = 0)         AS issue_free_opportunity_s,
  sumIf(content_intent_s, content_issues != 0)         AS impacted_opportunity_s,
  sumIf(content_intent_s, bitTest(content_issues, 1))  AS rebuffer_impacted_s,
  sumIf(content_intent_s, bitTest(content_issues, 0))  AS startup_impacted_s,
  sumIf(content_intent_s, bitTest(content_issues, 3))  AS error_impacted_s,

  count()                                                AS minutes,

FROM experience_minute
GROUP BY minute_ts, context_key, policy_key;

One quantity, two names

Intent seconds at record level are opportunity seconds at KPI level. Same measurement: the SDK records what the viewer was trying to watch; the KPI layer calls it the opportunity that was either honoured or impacted. The cube column names use the KPI vocabulary so nothing has to be translated between this page and the specification.

issue_free_opportunity_s and impacted_opportunity_s operate on eligible seconds under the recorded policy_key. The bits in content_issues were already set on-device according to that policy's eligibility floor, intent-time rule and thresholds. "Issue bit set = bad" is the simpler and wrong reading; the cube inherits the semantics, it does not define them.

Which is why eligibility and KPI definitions live in one shared metric layer, linked by the detector and wrapped by the query service. Implemented twice, they drift — and the failure looks like an alert firing at 22.4% while the on-call dashboard shows 4%.

Why this is the whole architecture

Attribution across 10 dimensions every 30 seconds is a set of group-bys over 42,000 rows. That is trivial. The same query over 1M per-playback rows is not. Cardinality discipline in the context tuple is not a byte-saving trick — it is what makes the detector possible at all.

Retention asymmetry

Cube rows have no identifiers and survive for years at ~0.73 GB/day, roughly 265 GB per year. Raw rows carry identifiers, cost 10.7 GB/day, and expire at 21 days. Anything you did not put in the cube schema is a question you cannot ask about last year.

The services lane: the same minute, from behind the CDN

Stages 01–08 tell you that viewers on edge-b were hurt. They cannot tell you what was happening inside your own delivery chain while it happened. The services lane applies the SDK's discipline to backend telemetry: an OTel agent on every node compiles spans into minute records, so the incident can be read on both sides of the same clock. Components marked proposed do not exist upstream and would be built.

The same scaling problem, one layer down

Span volume is a property of the call graph, not of the viewing. Adding a child span to the packager doubles its trace bill and changes nothing about whether a viewer rebuffered — the argument from stage 01, restated for services.

Head sampling breaks the summary

If services head-sample at 10%, the agent sees 10% of requests and every count it produces is a sample. Critical-path services run always-on, and the summary is computed from 100% of spans before any sampling decision. Where that is impossible, the record carries OTel adjusted counts and is flagged, never silently treated as complete.

An empty minute is not a healthy minute

An agent that handled zero requests still sends a sealed envelope with its sequence number. A missing envelope is a coverage gap — a dead node, a partitioned pod — and is reported as one. Why not exponential histograms

OTLP's exponential histograms are compact and mergeable, but they rescale per stream. Two streams at different scales do not share boundaries, and neither shares them with the client ladder. Fixed explicit bounds from one ladder is what lets a client segment-download bucket and a server response bucket mean the same interval.

Stagger, smaller

Hundreds of agents, not millions of devices, so a 10-second spread is enough. Same rule as stage 05: the offset hashes the agent id, never a region or edge, so a collector hiccup cannot impersonate a regional incident.

Detection: two tests that never gate each other

backend · analysis

The detector reads the cube. It runs a seasonal-baseline comparison and a mix-shift comparison, independently, every 30 seconds.

Signal against baseline

Cohort edge-b · app 7.2.0, content issue rate — the complement of Issue-Free Opportunity %. The seasonal baseline is modelled on time-of-week, platform class, geography and live-event state, with sparse cohorts pooled toward their parent. A flat baseline would fire every evening at prime time; a trailing average would absorb this step and never fire.

detector query · runs every 30s over the cube
SELECT
  c.context_key,
  sum(c.impacted_opportunity_s) / sum(c.opportunity_s)  AS observed_rate,
  any(b.expected_rate)                                   AS expected_rate,
  sum(c.opportunity_s)                                   AS opportunity_s,
  (observed_rate - expected_rate) * opportunity_s         AS excess_impacted_s
FROM minute_cube c
JOIN baseline_seasonal b USING (context_key, tow_bucket, policy_key)
WHERE minute_ts BETWEEN '12:09:00' AND '12:13:00'       -- sealed window
  AND b.support_minutes > 5000                             -- no baseline, no alert
GROUP BY c.context_key
HAVING excess_impacted_s > 50000 AND opportunity_s > 200000  -- floors
ORDER BY excess_impacted_s DESC;

The join key has a delivery cost

Putting sid on segment requests as a query argument changes the CDN cache key unless the CDN is configured to ignore it — which would turn every segment into a miss. Sending it as a header avoids that but triggers CORS preflights on web players. Decide per platform, alongside the CMCD privacy review already open in the specification.

Partial traces

A trace crosses several nodes, and each agent holds only its own spans. An agent that missed the directive, restarted, or aged the spans out leaves a hole in the reassembled trace. Holes are marked, not papered over, and buffer length is sized from measured OpAMP propagation latency, not a guess.

What this cannot retrieve

Viewers who quit at 12:12 never send another batch and never receive the directive. The forensic sample is therefore biased toward the tolerant. Continuous low-rate baseline sampling and the universal PlaybackAttempt stream reduce that bias; nothing eliminates it.

FROM read_parquet('s3://qoe-archive/minute_cube/date=2026-0[6-9]-*/*/*') c
JOIN read_parquet('s3://qoe-archive/dims/context_dim.parquet') d
     USING (context_key)
WHERE d.cdn = 'edge-b';               -- 90 days, ~4 GB, once

SELECT date_trunc('hour', minute_ts) AS hr, app_version,
       impacted_rate(impacted_opportunity_s, opportunity_s) AS rate
FROM edgeb GROUP BY ALL
HAVING rate > 0.08 ORDER BY hr;

Illustrative output. Two earlier edge-b episodes, both on live-match evenings, both below the paging floor. The pattern was in the data before the incident; nobody had asked. impacted_rate is a macro from the pack — sum over sum, never an average of averages.

ClickHouse keeps the pager

The detector, dashboards, incident drill-down and the aligned view read ClickHouse. Exploration — by people or by an analytics agent — reads the archive. The two never compete for CPU, and the cluster is sized for monitoring rather than for curiosity.

What it costs

About 13 GB of Parquet a day; ~5 TB for thirteen months on object storage, roughly $100–120 a month at list price. The working set for a question like this one is a few gigabytes. Planning arithmetic, to be replaced with shadow-phase measurements.

Same numbers, or no numbers

The agent and the analyst compute KPIs through the same versioned macro pack as the dashboards, and the pack refuses to mix policy versions. Sample by a hash of the archive key, never by row, or per-playback questions silently break. A laptop outside the cloud region pays egress; heavy users run DuckDB on an in-region VM.

The one sentence

Measure exactly on the device and at every service node, reduce semantically at the same minute boundary, aggregate both to bounded cubes at write time, detect against mix-aware baselines on what viewers experienced, corroborate against what the services were doing in the same minute, rank by absolute impact, and go get the raw evidence — player timelines and traces — only for the cohort that matters — then let anyone, or any agent, question the history on a laptop without touching the system that pages people.
