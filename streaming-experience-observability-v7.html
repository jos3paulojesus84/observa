Streaming Experience Observability

A population-scale quality-of-experience system that measures playback where the truth is, aggregates where the scale is, and retrieves raw evidence only where it is needed — and lines viewer impact up, minute for minute, with what the services behind it were doing. This document is the implementation contract.

Contents

    0 Summary

    Event-centric quality-of-experience analytics reconstruct each viewing session on the server from a stream of raw player events. That model scales with events per session, which is the wrong quantity: it is large, it varies by platform and player version, and almost none of it is read.

    This architecture scales the hot path with viewing minutes instead. The player SDK runs a deterministic state machine, measures exact timings locally, and compiles them into three small semantic records. The backend aggregates those records into a bounded cube, detects population change against mix-aware baselines, and attributes impact to cohorts. Raw session timelines are sampled continuously at a low rate and captured at higher rate on demand, as evidence rather than as the analytical substrate.

    Two supporting records carry state that would otherwise be repeated on every row: ContextDefinition (§5) and ForensicPolicy (§11).

    1 Positioning

    This is not a general replacement for streaming analytics. It is a specific claim about which layer should be universal and which should be sampled.

    1.1 What this does differently

    • Cost scales with watch time, not player chattiness. Hot-path volume is a function of concurrent viewers, and is unaffected by a player release that doubles its event rate.
    • Attribution ranks by impact, not by rate. The worst-performing cohort is usually small and irrelevant. The system reports the cohort explaining the most excess degradation, with traffic share attached.
    • The measurement contract is explicit and versioned. Eligibility, taxonomy, and thresholds are named policies carried on every record, so a KPI series cannot silently change meaning underneath a dashboard.
    • Dashboards never depend on raw retrieval. The forensic store can be slow, cheap, and small without affecting operational monitoring.
    • Viewer impact and service behaviour on one clock. Players and critical-path service nodes both reduce to wall-clock minutes over the same conformed dimensions, so "who was hurt, where, by what, how many" and "what our services were doing at that moment" are one join, not two products and a war room (§12).
    • Exploration runs off the monitoring cluster. Ad-hoc and historical analysis reads a Parquet archive in DuckDB, so it costs object storage and a laptop rather than ClickHouse capacity sized for peak curiosity (§8.9).

    1.2 What established vendors do better

    An honest comparison, because reviewers will ask and because these gaps are real.

    • Full session-level forensics on every session, out of the box, with no sampling decision to get wrong.
    • Cross-publisher benchmarking, which requires a customer base this system does not have.
    • A mature integration surface: player plugins across dozens of SDK versions, ad-verification ecosystems, CDN and encoder correlation.
    • Full-fidelity distributed tracing across every service. APM products trace everything; the services lane summarises only the critical delivery path and keeps raw spans as evidence.
    • Arbitrary retrospective re-analysis. Once a threshold is applied on-device, history cannot be recomputed against a different threshold (§13.3).

    1.3 Out of scope

    This system does not replace player debug logs, CDN access logs, ad verification, encoder monitoring, general-purpose APM, or business analytics. The services lane (§12) summarises the critical delivery path — origin shield, packager, license, entitlement, ad decisioning — for correlation with viewer impact; it is not a tracing product for every service, and engineers still debug code in their APM. It correlates with these systems; it does not subsume them.

    3 Measurement model

    3.1 Why three primitives

    A minute abstraction alone cannot represent a session that never produced a minute, and it cannot answer how many seconds of a specific ad were watched. Rather than distort the minute to cover both, acquisition and consumption get their own records. Each primitive has one job and a stable key.

    3.2 Identity and boundaries

    Three identifiers, deliberately separate. Getting this wrong is the most expensive schema mistake available, because multiview and picture-in-picture put two concurrent players inside one session.

    The minute carries two intent budgets and two bitmaps, not one bitmap and a seconds split. Splitting only the denominator lets you compute a content-only denominator with an ad-contaminated numerator, which produces content incidents every time the ad server degrades. Two bitmaps cost roughly eight bytes and make content QoE and ad QoE independently computable from a single record.

    3.5 Eligibility

    Normative

    A minute is eligible when intent_seconds ≥ min_intent or when the minute contains an observed experience issue during playback intent. Paused and stopped time is excluded. The override exists because a viewer who obtained three seconds of video in a minute of continuous rebuffering is having the worst experience the system can measure, and an eligibility floor based on successful playback would delete exactly that minute from the denominator.

    Both intent_seconds and active_seconds are stored so the denominator is auditable after the fact and so a change of policy can be reasoned about rather than guessed at.

    Two watch-time numbers, on purpose

    Interval watch time will not equal summed minute active_seconds. Intervals measure playback; minutes apply eligibility rules and exclude paused time. They measure different things deliberately. Publish one definition of each in the metric layer and state the divergence, because otherwise it gets filed as a bug in month two.

    3.7 Why buckets, and where they stop

    Durations are carried as log-scale bucket indices rather than exact values. The byte saving is secondary. The real property is that buckets are additive and exact values are not: a cube row can hold "seconds in bucket 0…15" and two rows merge by summing, whereas exact milliseconds can only yield a percentile through a quantile sketch stored per row, which would run to hundreds of bytes and dwarf a 60-byte cube row.

    So the comparison is not one byte against four. It is "the cube can answer this" against "every percentile query falls through to the raw tier". The pattern throughout: the record carries a bucket index, the cube carries counts per bucket, and percentiles come out of the histogram.

    Averaging bucket indices, or averaging per-minute averages, is wrong and will look plausible: it weights a one-minute viewer the same as a three-hour one. Bucket midpoints introduce roughly ±4% error on the mean — fine for CDN cost trending, not fine for anything contractual.

    4.1 Why intent seconds, and what it costs

    Using active playback seconds as the denominator excludes the time a viewer spends unsuccessfully trying to watch, which is the time that matters most. Using intent seconds makes catastrophic buffering visible.

    The cost is that heavy buffering inflates the denominator as well as the numerator, which compresses the rate during severe incidents. Two consequences follow, and both are binding:

    Normative

    The rate is never presented alone. Impacted Opportunity Seconds accompanies it in every operational view.

    Cohort ranking uses absolute excess impacted seconds, never rate. A badly degraded cohort carries an inflated denominator, so rate systematically understates the worst cohorts relative to healthy ones. Ranking by rate would bias attribution away from the cohorts it exists to find.

    Issue-Free Opportunity % and its complement are the same number. Only one appears as a headline.

    5.1 Client-observable versus server-derived

    The split matters far beyond byte counts, and it determines what forensic targeting can do (§11.2).

    Client-observableServer-derived at ingest
    Device class, OS and version, app version, player version, protocol, DRM system, requested CDN hostname, connection typeISP and ASN, country / region / city, resolved CDN and edge, client IP class. Derived from the connection; never sent by the client and never pushed back to it.

    5.2 Cardinality discipline

    The context tuple is a cross-product, so every dimension added to it multiplies. Content ID lives on PlaybackInterval, not in the context tuple: it is the highest-cardinality field available and the one that changes most often.

    This is not only a payload concern. Distinct contexts per minute set the width of the aggregation cube (§8.2), which sets whether cohort attribution can run every window. Context cardinality is therefore a hard budget with an enforced limit and a defined fallback, not a guideline.

    6.3 Permanent stalls are not rebuffers

    Nobody sits through sixty seconds of continuous buffering. Counting it as a rebuffer makes rebuffer statistics mean something other than what every reader assumes, which is "recoverable interruptions".

    Normative — two halves, both required

    Reclassify. Continuous buffering past the stall threshold sets STALL_TIMEOUT and clears REBUFFER for that minute. Playback transitions to a terminal STALLED_OUT state and emits a terminal record with end_reason = STALL_TIMEOUT.

    Cap intent accrual at the threshold plus a short grace, then stop counting. Without this, a viewer who walked away from a spinning player accrues sixty impacted seconds every minute for as long as the app stays open. Those sessions would dominate the excess-impact ranking in §10 and point every incident at whichever cohort leaves televisions running.

    The by-product is a mid-playback abandonment rate, distinct from the startup ABANDONED in §3.3, and often a better quality signal than rebuffer rate. Cost: one spare bit in the existing bitmap and a state-machine change. Zero additional bytes.

    Normative

    CDN_SWITCH is not in the issue bitmap. It belongs in context_change_flags. This preserves the invariant that a non-zero issue bitmap means the minute was impacted, so a consumer computing "any bit set" cannot accidentally classify a routine CDN failover as a quality incident.

    7 Time, ordering, and completeness

    • Minute buckets are UTC wall-clock aligned, so simultaneous impact across unrelated viewers lands in the same bucket. This is what makes the hero figure detectable and what makes live-event detection work.
    • Client timestamps are treated as measurements. Ingest records server receipt time and the estimated client offset, and normalizes.
    • Offset is estimated from the round trip on the first successful upload. Until then the client's own clock is used and the records are flagged as un-normalized. A lightweight time sync at SDK init removes this gap for the first batch, which is the startup batch.
    • Upload cadence is one batch per minute, staggered per playback (§7.2). Targeted forensic playbacks drop to 10 seconds.
    • Allowed lateness is explicit and bounded (§8.8), and is shorter than it would otherwise be because the stagger makes arrival times deterministic.

    7.1 Bucket alignment and upload timing are different knobs

    They are easy to conflate, and earlier versions specified only the first. Bucket alignment decides which sixty seconds of playback aggregate into one record. Upload timing decides when the device transmits. Alignment must be wall-clock; transmission must not be.

    Why alignment cannot be session-relative

    If one viewer's minute runs 12:11:00–12:12:00 and another's runs 12:11:23–12:12:23, an incident at 12:11:30 falls inside one bucket for the first and smears across two for the second. Synchronised impact is the detection signal, and misalignment dilutes it exactly when it matters.

    Two further consequences: the cube aggregates on GROUP BY minute_ts, and session-relative epochs leave nothing to group on; and it cannot be repaired server-side, because the sub-second detail needed to re-split a minute across wall-clock boundaries is discarded at reduction time. Cross-sectional live-event detection (§9.3) depends on alignment too.

    7.2 Upload scheduling

    Sealing every record on the wall-clock boundary and transmitting immediately would put a million requests into a narrow burst each minute, with collectors idle between bursts.

    Normative

    A record is sealed on the wall-clock boundary and uploaded at minute_end + (hash(playback_id) mod 60) seconds. Buckets stay aligned; request load flattens to the steady record rate.

    The stagger is stable per playback, so a device keeps its slot and the ACK-targeting cadence stays predictable. It derives from playback_id alone and never from a cohort-correlated field — hash the ISP and every subscriber of one provider uploads together, at which point a collector wobble in a single slot is indistinguishable from an incident at that ISP.

    The latency cost is close to self-financing. Event-to-upload becomes the sum of two uniform waits, raising the p95 flush term from 60s to roughly 102s. But a deterministic stagger tells the server exactly when a minute's records should have arrived, so allowed lateness falls from 45s to about 20s. The revised budget in §8.7 lands at 292s, still inside the five-minute target.

    7.3 Priority reporting

    Two different problems hide under one label, and only one of them is about speed.

    CaseProblemVerdict
    Flush on abnormal terminationOn web and CTV the process may not survive to the next cadence tick, so a fatal-error or stall-out record is not delayed — it is lostAlways. Cheap, and a durability fix rather than a latency one
    Priority upload for latencySixty seconds off a 292s budget is 20%, and confirmation still dominatesOnly paired with fast-path detection. Neither half is worth much alone

    Priority earns its place when a catastrophic signal — playback impossible across an entire cohort, a DRM outage, a release that cannot start — is permitted to alert on a single window rather than two. Confirmation then stops dominating and upload latency becomes the leading term. That exception is a deliberate, named relaxation of §8.7's confirmation rule, not a general loosening of it.

    Mandatory safeguards, not optional hardening

    A global CDN failure makes millions of devices conclude "critical" simultaneously and upload at once, which is a self-inflicted denial of service against the collectors during an incident. Priority therefore carries 0–5s jitter regardless, a per-device rate limit, and a server-side kill switch in the ACK so the channel can be shed under load. Priority records use the same dedupe and idempotency path — it is a flag, never a second pipeline.

    Which conditions qualify for priority is a field in the classification ruleset (§14), so the channel can be narrowed or disabled globally without an app release.

    7.4 Completeness is a measured quantity

    Poor networks cause both bad playback and failed uploads, so missing telemetry is correlated with the exact conditions being measured. Treating absence as health would bias every KPI in the optimistic direction.

    Normative

    Every minute record carries a monotonic sequence_no. A terminal record bounds the expected tail of each playback. The backend computes a completeness ratio from sequence gaps and unterminated playbacks, and reports it broken out by platform tier, network class, region, and ISP. A single aggregate completeness number is not permitted, because the entire point is that loss is non-uniform.

    8.3 Access paths: how a drill-down actually resolves

    ClickHouse has no per-column index in the transactional sense. Nothing maps cdn = 'edge-b' to row locations. The sort key is the index, it is sparse — one entry per granule of 8,192 rows — and it accelerates only filters on a prefix of it. So (minute_ts, context_key, playback_id) accelerates a time filter, then a single context_key within that time, and nothing else.

    One consequence deserves stating plainly, because it is easy to assume otherwise. context_key is a surrogate, so a cohort such as "all of edge-b" spans thousands of scattered key values and resolves to many small ranges rather than one contiguous read. Sorting still earns its place through compression and locality; it does not turn a cohort query into a range scan.

    8.5 Audience and engagement, without a second source of truth

    Watch time per title, completion rate, and unique viewers come from the interval and attempt tiers through a daily rollup, with unique counts from a device_id sketch. They are not produced by a client-emitted session summary.

    Normative

    A client session summary is lost exactly when sessions end badly — app killed, television unplugged, process terminated. Audience numbers built on it would be biased low in precisely the cohorts having problems, which is the same survivorship bias this architecture fights everywhere else, reintroduced at the business-reporting layer.

    If a terminal session record is emitted, it has the job sequence_no has for minutes: completeness checking, never metric production. It tells you whether all intervals arrived. It never feeds a published number.

    8.6 Query serving

    Dashboard queries hit the cube and return in interactive time. Drill-down that falls below cube granularity — a specific asset, a specific device model, a free-text dimension — routes to the raw minute tier and is surfaced in the interface as a slower path with its own progress state, never as a blocked dashboard.

    8.7 Latency budget

    "Near real time" is not a design input. A budget that sums to the SLO is. Every component below has a ceiling, and the ceilings add up.

    • Seal wait + upload stagger (p95)102s
    • Upload, ingest, resolution10s
    • Aggregation window + lateness20s
    • Detector evaluation cadence30s
    • Confirmation (2 windows)120s
    • Alert dispatch10s
    • Onset to alert, p95292s ≈ 4.9 min

    Target: p95 onset-to-alert under five minutes, with roughly 8 seconds of headroom.

    The stagger in §7.2 raises the flush term and lowers allowed lateness, because deterministic arrival times let the window close sooner. The trade is near neutral and buys flat collector load. The headroom is now thin. Any further latency spend needs a corresponding saving, and the obvious candidate — shortening confirmation — trades directly against false alerts.

    The flush interval is set to one minute, matching the minute cadence of the records themselves. A five-minute batch would be markedly cheaper — the header amortises across five records and compression has more to work with — but it would place a five-minute floor under detection and consume the entire budget before the detector ran once. Cadence is where bytes are traded for latency, and §17 prices the trade.

    Design consequence

    Confirmation is the dominant term, at 41% of the budget. Confirmation is also the only defence against alerting on one noisy window. The detection-latency SLO and the false-positive budget are therefore the same dial, and any proposal to tighten the SLO is a proposal to accept more false alerts. Treat them as one decision, made once, with both numbers written down.

    8.8 Sealed windows and restatement

    Late data — an offline queue flushing hours later — retroactively changes historical aggregates. Without a rule, the number an operator saw during an incident will not match the number in the postmortem, and nobody will know which was wrong.

    Normative

    Alerting operates on sealed windows: window close plus allowed lateness, evaluated once, never revisited. Alert history is immutable.

    Reporting may restate. Restated windows are marked as such in the interface, and the restatement delta is itself a monitored quantity, because a growing delta means delivery is degrading.

    9 Baselines, mix shift, and detection

    9.1 Two tests, always independent

    Normative

    The mix-shift test and the within-cohort regression test run independently and neither gates the other. A staged app rollout shifts the population toward the new version and, if the new version is worse, degrades it. Those are the same event. A rule that classifies degradation as "mix shift, not regression" when mix adjustment absorbs it will suppress alerts on the single most common cause of real regressions.

    • Mix-shift test: did the population composition change materially against the reference distribution?
    • Within-cohort test: did each material cohort move against its own expected baseline?
    • Both findings are reported. "The v7.2 cohort grew from 4% to 31% of traffic and its own issue rate rose 2.4 points" is one sentence and two independent facts.
    • The aggregate KPI is additionally compared against a mix-adjusted counterfactual so operators can see how much of a headline move is composition.

    9.2 Baseline construction

    Streaming traffic is strongly seasonal by time of day, day of week, and event schedule. A flat baseline fires every evening; a trailing average absorbs slow degradation and never fires.

    • Baselines are seasonal and cohort-aware, modelled on time-of-week, platform class, geography, and live-event state.
    • Sparse cohorts use hierarchical partial pooling, borrowing strength from their parent cohort rather than estimating from a handful of noisy minutes. A small ISP in a small region at 3am has no reliable baseline of its own.
    • Baseline versions are retained so historical alerts remain reproducible.
    Normative

    No adequate baseline, no historical alert. Cross-sectional detection remains available in that case.

    9.3 Large live events

    A championship-scale event has no historical precedent to baseline against, and it is the day operators most need detection. The technique changes rather than degrading.

    Cross-sectional detection: compare each cohort against concurrent cohorts within the same event window, controlling for stable dimensions, instead of against its own history. Every viewer is watching the same content at the same moment, which makes the comparison unusually clean, and wall-clock-aligned minutes make synchronized population impact visible directly.

    10 Cohort attribution

    The objective is the cohort explaining the most excess degradation, not the cohort with the worst rate. Those are rarely the same cohort, and the second one is rarely actionable.

    excess impact = (cohort observed rate − cohort expected baseline rate) × cohort opportunity seconds
    • Hierarchical one-dimension-at-a-time drill-down, with bounded interaction testing only where a single dimension leaves substantial excess unexplained.
    • Minimum traffic floor and minimum excess-impact floor, both configured.
    • Persistence across N consecutive windows, unless severity warrants immediate escalation.
    • Multiple-comparison control across the candidate cohort set, because ten dimensions with interactions is thousands of hypotheses per window.
    • Ranking uses absolute excess seconds, per §4.1.

    CDN, ISP, region, and device are heavily correlated in practice. The system reports contributing cohorts and never claims causation from an aggregate alone.

    Example output

    "CDN B on app version 7.x explains 38% of excess rebuffer impact while carrying 7% of viewing opportunity." Every clause is measured. None of them says why.

    Mechanism is established by pulling representative sessions from the forensic tier and reading their timelines, which is the point of §11.

    11 Forensics and targeting

    11.1 Evidence budget

    • Baseline sampling: a small deterministic session-level sample runs continuously. Because it is unconditional, it captures incident onset and it captures viewers who abandoned before any targeting could reach them.
    • Adaptive sampling: on detection, raw capture increases for the selected cohort until the investigation window expires.
    • Onset buffer: a bounded circular raw-event buffer on the client, initial target five minutes, sized to the measured detection-and-targeting latency from §8.7 rather than to a guess.
    • Selection is session-level and deterministic, so a chosen session yields a coherent timeline rather than a scatter of unrelated events.

    11.2 Targeting rides the upload ACK

    Cohort predicates are written over dimensions like ISP, region, and resolved CDN edge. A client cannot evaluate those predicates, because it does not know its own ISP or region — they are derived from the connection at ingest. Pushing derived attributes down to clients to enable local matching would add a round trip, add a privacy surface, and duplicate server state.

    Normative

    Predicates are evaluated server-side at ingest, against dimensions the server already resolved. The batch upload response carries the targeting directive. The client flushes its rolling buffer and switches to detailed capture on receipt.

    Consequences: targeting latency is bounded by one batch interval; no separate control channel or polling loop is needed; and a targeted playback can drop to a shorter batch interval to tighten the loop further.

    A viewer who abandons within seconds of an incident may never upload another batch, and so never receives a targeting directive. Continuous baseline sampling and the universal PlaybackAttempt signal reduce this bias. They do not eliminate it. The most impacted sessions remain the hardest to retrieve, and forensic samples should be read with that in mind.

    12 Services lane: the same minute, from behind the CDN

    Everything up to §11 establishes who was impacted, where, by which issue, and how much. None of it says what the publisher's own delivery chain was doing at the time. Today that answer lives in a different product — an APM or tracing vendor, priced on hosts and ingested spans, with its own clock, its own names and its own retention — and the two are joined by people comparing screenshots in an incident channel. The services lane closes that gap by applying the SDK's discipline to backend OpenTelemetry: measure exactly at the node, reduce semantically at the same wall-clock minute, aggregate to a bounded cube, and keep raw spans on the node unless they are selected as evidence.

    12.3 Conformed dimensions are the join

    Normative

    The client lane resolves the CDN edge from the connection at ingest (§5.1); the services lane reads it from a header the CDN injects on origin fetch. If one says edge-b7 and the other b7.chi.cdnprovider.net, the join returns nothing and looks like health. Both resolve into one owned dim_edge and dim_region. An unmapped value is counted and alerted, never dropped.

    Only conformed dimensions cross: edge, region, and through them CDN. App version, device and ISP are client-only; service version and pod are server-only. The aligned view therefore answers "which service path moved with this edge's viewers", not "which service hurt app 7.2.0". Content identity is in neither cube, by design (§5.2).

    12.4 The service cube

    Summaries for the same key and minute merge across agents by summation into one row — about 4,800 rows a minute and 0.28 GB a day, small enough to keep for months beside the minute cube. Latency quantiles come out of the summed ladder at rung resolution, roughly ±12% on any quantile, stated rather than hidden. Per-agent coverage lives in its own table, one row per agent per minute.

    12.5 The aligned view

    Because both lanes share the minute and the conformed keys, putting them side by side is a join on coordinates — no trace ids, no viewer ids, no sampling. Every alert from §10 carries the service paths that moved with the contributing cohort in the same sealed window, how far they moved, and which moved first.

    The services lane corroborates; it never triggers. QoE pages come only from the client lane (§9, §10). The service cube keeps its own SLO alerting on its own units, and requests are never added to opportunity seconds.

    No claim of cause. Two aligned series moving together with one leading is much stronger evidence than a cohort alone, and still correlation over aggregates. The alert says "moved with", never "caused by".

    It costs nothing in latency. A service minute lands about 20 seconds after its minute ends, against a client p95 of 102 seconds, so the matching service rows are already waiting when the client window seals and the join adds nothing to the 292-second budget (§8.7).

    12.6 Evidence and completeness

    One policy, two channels. The control plane fans a ForensicPolicy out to the upload ACK for players (§11.2) and over OpAMP to agents. Each agent keeps a five-minute rolling span buffer on local disk, flushes the spans matching the policy, and the forensic tier reassembles traces by trace_id. A CMCD session id on targeted playbacks links a player's timeline to the exact requests that served it; that mapping exists only inside the access-audited forensic tier (§20, question 6). Holes left by agents that missed the directive or restarted are marked, not papered over.

    Normative — an empty minute is not a healthy minute

    An agent that handled no requests still sends a sealed envelope with its sequence number. A missing envelope is a coverage gap — a dead node, a partitioned pod — named per agent and reported per cluster and region, never pooled. Without this rule, the node that crashed under the incident is exactly the node whose errors disappear. Where coverage is partial, the aligned view marks service counts as lower bounds.

    12.7 What it costs, and what it does not replace

    At 1M concurrent the planning case is ~140,000 critical-path spans a second across ~320 nodes: ~14.6 MB/s compressed if shipped raw. Minute summaries are ~23 KB/s, and a 0.5% evidence baseline ~73 KB/s. Summary volume scales with distinct keys rather than requests, so a per-agent cardinality budget — default 150 keys per minute, overflow folded into _other and monitored — is the services-lane counterpart of §5.2.

    Known limitations

    It does not replace general APM: engineers still need traces across every service to debug code. What it removes is the need to ship and index every critical-path span to answer the incident question, and the need for a human to align two products' clocks and naming while viewers are failing. Visibility also stops at the publisher's own services — the CDN's internals, the ISP and the last mile are seen only through the client lane and, where available, CDN logs joined on the same conformed edge. A clean services lane during a viewer incident is itself a finding — "beyond our services" — not an absence of one.

    13 Configuration, schema, and series integrity

    13.1 Configuration is a production risk surface

    Remote configuration sets thresholds that every client applies locally. A startup threshold mistakenly pushed at 100ms would flag essentially every minute worldwide and collapse the KPI, with no code change and no deploy.

    • Client-side sanity bounds on every remote value; out-of-range values are rejected locally and the rejection is reported as telemetry.
    • Staged rollout by platform, app version, and region before global release.
    • Every configuration carries a version, effective time, owner, and audit trail, with a fast rollback path.
    • Remote config changes classification. It never changes state machine topology or the observation vocabulary, both of which are fixed at release and heavily tested across platforms. §14 defines exactly where that line falls.

    13.2 Series compatibility

    Normative

    A KPI series is homogeneous only when schema version, taxonomy version, eligibility policy, and threshold profile are compatible across it. Aggregators refuse to combine incompatible profiles. A profile change creates a visible series boundary, or records are explicitly normalized to a common policy.

    13.3 What cannot be recomputed

    Thresholds are applied on-device at collection time. Re-thresholding history is possible only within the resolution retained in issue_counters; a boolean flag cannot be recovered into a duration that was never transmitted. This is the real and permanent cost of edge classification, and it is why counters carry bucketed durations rather than bare booleans. Say it plainly in any comparison against event-centric systems, which can re-derive history at will.

    14 Remote classification rulesets

    Classification logic will be wrong somewhere, and a meaningful share of the fleet is connected-TV hardware that updates slowly or never. A misclassification that can only be fixed by shipping an app release is therefore a permanently wrong metric on the devices that perform worst — which is exactly where the measurement matters most.

    The useful boundary is not parameters against logic. It is whether a change can be expressed over observations the SDK already collects.

    14.1 Three tiers

    TierExamplesNeeds a release?
    1 — ParametersThresholds, eligibility floor, stall timeout, bucket boundaries, sample ratesNo (§13)
    2 — Declarative rulesWhich condition sets which bit, reclassifying an issue, adding a bit in spare bitmap space, per-platform condition overrides, changing how a counter is derivedNo — this section
    3 — New inputsA new observation in the vocabulary, subscribing to a player event never listened to, freeze detection on a new TV OS, transport or record-type changesYes

    Roughly three quarters of post-launch classification changes fall in tiers 1 and 2. The remainder need new inputs, and no configuration mechanism can rescue them.

    14.2 The observation vocabulary decides everything

    The evaluator can only reason over what the SDK measures, so the vocabulary shipped at release is the hard ceiling on what can ever be changed remotely.

    state, prev_state, state_duration_ms, buffering_ms_continuous, buffering_count,

    Ship the vocabulary deliberately wider than currently needed. Each additional observation costs almost nothing at runtime and buys a future rule change that needs no release. This decision is made once, at launch, and cannot be retrofitted into devices that will never update again.

    14.3 Ruleset schema

    The SDK ships a fixed evaluator. The rules are data.

    14.4 Why not push executable code

    A WebAssembly module or a JavaScript bundle would remove the tier-3 boundary altogether. It is the wrong trade here:

    • Platform and app-store policy on downloading executable code is restrictive and varies by store. Verify with legal rather than assuming.
    • Legacy CTV engines execute it poorly, and those are already the worst-measured devices in the fleet.
    • Remote code execution across millions of consumer devices is a security surface the organization then owns permanently.
    • The decisive one: it breaks §13.2. If classification can change arbitrarily, taxonomy_version and threshold_profile_id no longer guarantee that a KPI series means one thing, and the measuring instrument becomes version-indeterminate.

    A declarative ruleset keeps that guarantee, because a rule pack is a named, versioned, inspectable artifact rather than opaque behaviour.

    14.5 Shadow evaluation

    Normative

    A candidate ruleset is evaluated alongside the active one. The record is emitted under the active ruleset; a small disagreement summary is emitted beside it. A rule change is therefore validated against live production traffic before it is promoted, with no release and no corrupted series.

    The disagreement summary is also the conversion factor that bridges the series boundary at promotion, so history remains interpretable across the change. This is the Phase 1 shadow-mode discipline (§19) made permanent rather than temporary.

    14.6 Safety rails

    • ruleset_version on every record, carried in the batch header beside the existing policy versions — about two bytes per batch. Aggregators refuse to mix rulesets within one KPI series, exactly as they already refuse to mix threshold profiles (§13.2). A ruleset change creates a visible series boundary.
    • A known-good pack compiled into the binary. If remote configuration is unreachable, or every candidate fails validation, the SDK falls back to the ruleset it shipped with. A catastrophic configuration incident then degrades to "the semantics we released", never to "no telemetry".
    • Client-side validation before activation. The pack must parse, reference only known observations, and pass a bounded-complexity check — the evaluator must be total, with no loops and no allocation, because it runs continuously on weak CPUs. Rejections are reported as telemetry.
    • Signing, staged rollout by platform and region, and a kill switch, as §13.1 already mandates for thresholds. A ruleset is higher-blast-radius configuration, not a different kind of thing.
    Cost

    Roughly one engineering sprint for the evaluator and pack format, about two bytes per batch, and continuous evaluator CPU that must be measured on the weakest supported device during Phase 1. The expensive part is not the code. It is the discipline to ship a wide observation vocabulary at launch, when nobody yet knows which observations will turn out to matter.

    "Durable local queue where supported" quietly contains a large platform matrix. The platforms where durability is weakest are frequently the platforms where quality is worst, which is the dangerous direction for the bias to run.

    On legacy CTV the system measures less reliably than elsewhere, and it does so on the device class most likely to have quality problems. The mitigation is honesty rather than engineering: completeness is reported per tier (§7.4), and a coverage gap on a device class is never rendered in a way that could read as health on that device class.

    Queue overflow uses an explicit drop priority: attempt records and terminal records survive; minute records degrade oldest-first; interval records drop first. Every drop is counted and reported.

    17.4 Against buying instead

    The efficiency gain is not the business case

    No established vendor prices by the byte. Commercial QoE analytics is priced per peak concurrent viewer, per viewing hour, or per session, so telemetry efficiency never appears on the invoice. Against realistic vendor retention practice this design is roughly 20–30× on storage and 10–20× on infrastructure, and none of that is a discount on a licence.

    Fully loaded, building is usually more expensive than buying at this scale: infrastructure in the low hundreds of thousands annually plus six to ten engineers across SDKs, backend, data and on-call. A pitch built on telemetry savings ends at the first question about headcount.

    Build wins when two or more of these hold: the platform team already exists and this is marginal cost; vendor pricing is per viewing hour and watch time is very large; QoE must be fused with data that cannot leave the organization such as revenue, entitlement or churn; the semantics needed are not sold — impact-ranked attribution, the two independent tests, intent-time denominators, stall reclassification; multi-CDN delivery requires independent ground truth for accountability; or viewer QoE and service telemetry are today bought separately and correlated by hand. The efficiency then makes the build affordable to run, which is a different claim from it paying for itself.

    Read this as planning arithmetic, not a benchmark

    Every input above is an assumption. Real savings depend on the actual player event rate, payload sizes, compression ratios, batching, retention tiers, and raw sampling rate. Benchmark against captured production event streams during Phase 1 before any savings figure is quoted externally. The load-bearing claim is the change in scaling law — from events per session to viewing minutes — not any particular multiple.

    20 Open questions

    These are unresolved and each one blocks something. They are listed so review can close them rather than discover them during implementation.

    1 · Attempt definition on autoplay surfaces

    Browse and feed surfaces autoplay aggressively. Whether those count as playback attempts changes Playback Start Success % by a large factor and is a product decision, not an engineering one. Blocks: KPI baseline, Phase 2 targets.

    Resolved since v4

    QUALITY_DEGRADATION stays out of the headline KPI. Quality is now measured rather than flagged — bitrate buckets and peak-rendition seconds (§3.7) give a real quality metric, so the bit no longer has to carry that weight. Ad and content separation is resolved by asset_type on intervals plus the two bitmaps. Permanent stalls are resolved in §6.3.

    2 · Ladder granularity

    Time at peak rendition is one byte. Seconds at each rung of the ladder would be about eight, and would answer encoding-ladder questions a single mean cannot — a viewer oscillating between 5800 and 1600 kbps averages to a number nobody experienced. Worth the bytes only if ladder decisions are actually made from this data. Blocks: quality cube schema.

    3 · Observation vocabulary scope

    Which observations ship in the first SDK (§14.2). Too narrow and future rule changes need releases the CTV fleet will not take; too wide and the evaluator carries dead weight on weak CPUs. This cannot be retrofitted to non-updating devices, so it is decided once. Blocks: SDK v1 scope, and it is irreversible.

    4 · Context cardinality budget

    The cube width depends on distinct contexts per minute on real traffic. This has not been measured. If it is much larger than expected, dimensions move out of the context tuple. Blocks: cube design, §8.2 sizing.

    5 · Forensic tier contents

    Raw events, or reconstructed timelines written at capture time? Raw is cheaper to write and more faithful; reconstructed is far cheaper to read during an incident, which is when reading happens. Blocks: forensic storage design.

    6 · CMCD correlation key

    CMCD provides standardized client-to-CDN delivery context and is complementary rather than competing. Joining it to this data requires a shared session correlation key present in both CDN logs and telemetry, with its own privacy review. Blocks: CDN correlation workflows.

    7 · Multi-tenancy

    If this is offered to multiple publishers, isolation of config profiles, taxonomy versions, context dictionaries, and forensic access is unspecified. Blocks: anything beyond first-party use.

    8 · Combined ad and content headline number

    Executives will want one number. Combining content and ad QoE requires a weighting decision that is explicitly a product judgement. Blocks: executive dashboard.

    9 · Critical-path instrumentation and the edge header

    The services lane needs every critical-path service emitting OTel spans with a normalised route, and a CDN willing to inject its edge identifier on origin fetch. Coverage of both is unmeasured, and a CDN that will not send the header leaves only region as a join key. Blocks: services lane Phase 1.

    10 · Archive identity horizon

    A day-scoped re-keyed playback_id held for thirteen months. Within-day per-playback analysis needs it; privacy review may not allow it. The fallback is an archive of per-context-minute aggregates only, which keeps cohort history and loses per-playback questions. Blocks: archive schema.

    11 · Ownership of conformed dimensions

    dim_edge and dim_region become shared infrastructure between the video and platform teams. Someone owns the mapping, the unmapped-value alert, and the change when a CDN renames its edges. Blocks: aligned view in production.

    21 Implementation invariants

    If an implementation violates any of these, it is not this architecture.

    1. A failed start is never invisible because no viewing minute was created.
    2. A minute with playback intent and an observed issue is eligible and impacted, however little video played.
    3. issue_bitmap means experience impact and nothing else; context changes live in their own field.
    4. Content and ad experience are independently computable from a single minute record.
    5. Records are keyed on playback_id, so concurrent players never collide.
    6. Context registration never blocks first telemetry, and content identity is not in the context cross-product.
    7. Mix shift and within-cohort regression are evaluated independently and both are reported.
    8. No adequate baseline means no historical alert; cross-sectional detection remains available.
    9. Cohort ranking uses absolute excess impacted seconds, never rate.
    10. Forensic predicates are evaluated server-side; clients are never told their derived dimensions.
    11. Completeness is measured from sequence gaps and reported per platform tier, never pooled.
    12. Alerting reads sealed windows; restatements are visible and monitored.
    13. Every record carries enough version information to prevent a silent KPI discontinuity.
    14. Remote configuration is bounded, staged, auditable, and reversible.
    15. Minute buckets are wall-clock aligned; uploads are staggered by a hash of playback_id that is never cohort-correlated.
    16. Priority upload always carries jitter, a per-device rate limit, and a server-side kill switch.
    17. Remote rulesets change classification only. State machine topology and the observation vocabulary are fixed at release.
    18. Every record carries ruleset_version, and aggregators refuse to mix rulesets within one series.
    19. A known-good ruleset ships inside the binary and is the fallback whenever remote configuration is unreachable or invalid.
    20. Identifiers do not survive into warm or long-term tiers. The analytics archive carries only a day-scoped key that cannot be linked across days or to a device.
    21. No operational dashboard depends on raw-session retrieval.
    22. No query path depends on a per-row index. The cube is sized so that scanning it costs less than indexing it would.
    23. Continuous buffering past the stall threshold is STALL_TIMEOUT, not REBUFFER, and stops accruing intent seconds.
    24. Durations are bucketed where they must merge in a cube and exact where volume permits; bucket indices are never averaged.
    25. Audience and engagement derive from intervals and attempts. A client session summary is a completeness signal, never a metric source.
    26. Histogram counters live in the quality cube, never in the detector's cube.
    27. QoE alerts come only from the client lane. The services lane corroborates and never pages on viewer experience; requests are never added to opportunity seconds.
    28. Service summaries are computed from 100% of critical-path spans before sampling, on the shared ladder, with empty-minute envelopes. A missing agent is a coverage gap, never a zero.
    29. Client and service lanes join only on conformed dimensions; an unmapped edge is alerted, never dropped.
    30. No exploratory or agent workload runs on the monitoring cluster. It runs on the archive, with the same metric definitions.

    22 Design principle

    Observe everything cheaply. Detect population change honestly. Explain impact without claiming cause. Line viewers and services up on one clock. Keep evidence where evidence is needed.

    The architecture uses different primitives for acquisition, population experience, and exact consumption because no single abstraction represents all three without distorting at least one. The separation of universal observation from sampled evidence is what buys the scale, and the discipline about what each record can and cannot support is what makes the resulting numbers worth acting on.

    v7 extends the same discipline in two directions: behind the CDN, where service nodes reduce spans to minutes on the same clock and dimensions as the players, and after the fact, where the per-playback tiers land as Parquet that anyone — or any agent — can load and question without touching the system that pages people.
